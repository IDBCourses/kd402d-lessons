Recap and motivation · A different kind of input

A key and a pointer

A key

You press it. It's either down or it isn't.

It speaks only at the instant you press or let go. Between presses, there's nothing to hear.

Like knocking on a door: separate knocks, silence in between.

A pointer

It is always somewhere, even when your hand is still.

When it moves, the interesting thing isn't one moment. It's the path it travels, and how fast.

Like drawing with a finger in the air: one line, never "between".

No code on this slide, and none yet. Ask first, before revealing anything: what's the difference between using a key and using a mouse? Collect a few answers. Then draw out the two things that make a pointer different in kind. One: position is always available, even when nobody is doing anything. You can ask "where is it?" at any time; you can't ask a key "where is it?". Two: movement is information. Not only where you stopped, but how you got there, and how fast. Keep it lived experience: hands, not events.

what's detune?

              
A shift in a note's pitch, counted in cents. 100 cents is one key on the piano. 1200 cents is an octave. 0 is the note as it is.

            

          

        

        
Predict on laptops first. detune is new: it bends the note that's playing, without starting a new one. normX 0 gives 0 cents (the note as it is); normX 1 gives 1200 cents, an octave higher; the middle gives 600, half an octave. So left to right, the pitch slides up smoothly, one octave in total. Wrong answers get a hint. Next slide: hear it, and change the 1200.

Move 2: Speed

          
How much of the screen per second?

          
The event says where the pointer is. How fast it's going, we work out from two of them. In screens, not pixels, so it means the same on any screen.

        

        
11:05, about ten minutes. Speed isn't on the event: we derive it, the way Monday derived the gaps from the moments. Keep the last one, compare with this one. And we stay in normalised coordinates from Move 1: a speed of 1 means crossing the whole screen in one second, on a phone or a projector.

      


      
      

        Try it
        
Move 2: Speed

        

        
Keep the last one. Compare.

        

          

            

              
speed.jsMath.hypot is new

              

            

            
Math.hypot(dx, dy) gives the straight-line distance between two points: Pythagoras, done for you.

          

          

            

              
distance–screens

              
seconds–s

              
speed–screens per second

            

            
            
Move anywhere: slowly, then a fast flick.

          

        

        
Same shape as Monday's gaps: the last position and time live at the top (they have to survive between events, so State), and each pointermove compares the new one with them. Both positions are normalised, so the distance is in screens: 0.1 is a tenth of the screen. Divide by the time in seconds and speed is in screens per second. Move slowly: well under 1. Flick across: 3, 4, 5. Point at the trace: even a smooth hand gives a jumpy line, because each event is one tiny movement. Two honest edge cases: the very first move compares against 0, 0 at time 0, so it's nonsense (tiny); and if two moves arrive in the same millisecond, seconds is 0 and speed is Infinity. The demos skip those.

Edge to edge in half a second

        

          

            
You move the pointer straight across, from the left edge to the right edge, in half a second. What is speed?

            

            

          

          

            
speed.js

            

          

        

        
Everyone answers on their laptop. Left edge to right edge is the whole screen: a distance of 1. Half a second: 0.5. 1 / 0.5 = 2 screens per second. 0.5 is the common slip (dividing the wrong way round). 960 is what you'd get in pixels per ms on a 1920-wide screen, which is exactly the number we got rid of. In real code the distance comes in many small pieces, one per event, but the speed works out the same.

Move 3: Average over time

          
Don't trust one move. Average several.

          
Four ways to keep a history of speeds and average it. Each one fixes the last one's problem.

        

        
11:15, about twenty minutes. One movement is a tiny, jumpy piece of data. Averaging several cancels out the jumps. The question is which several: all of them, the last ten, or the ones from the last half second. Every slide in this move has the same picture: each stored speed is a bar, placed by how long ago it arrived, newest on the right. Bars that count towards the average are coloured; the line across is the average. Watch which bars count.

Move 3: Average over time · Version 3

        

        
Only keep 10 in the array

        

          

            

              
average.jsshift is new

              

            

            
push adds to the end. shift() takes the first item out: the oldest. (pop() would take the newest.)

          

          

            

            
            
speeds, newest on the right

            
0speeds.length

          

        

        
The easier way to get the last 10: never keep more than 10. After each push, if the array is longer than 10, shift() takes the oldest off the front. In at the back, out at the front, like a queue. Now the average is over the whole array, so the loop is the plain 0 to length one, and dividing by speeds.length means no special case while it fills up. Move: the boxes slide along, one in at the right, one out at the left, length stuck at 10. Same sound as version 2, a fraction of the memory. pop() would remove the newest, which is the opposite of what we want. Then: flick fast, and stop dead. Keep your hand still and watch the picture before going on.

      


      
      

        Your turn
        
Move 3: Average over time · Predict

        

        
Flick fast, then stop dead

        

          

            
With version 3, you flick the pointer fast across the screen, then hold your hand completely still. What does the average do?

            

            

          

          

            
remember

            

          

        

        
Everyone predicts. Most people expect it to drop to 0, because the pointer stopped. But nothing in the code runs when the pointer is still: no movement, no pointermove, no new speed, no shift. The last ten speeds were all fast, they stay in the array, so the average stays high and the note stays bent, forever, until you move again. Show it on the previous slide if anyone doubts it: the bars just slide left and stay coloured. A second, quieter problem: moving slowly gives fewer events, so ten speeds cover a longer stretch of time than ten fast ones. "The last 10" isn't a fixed amount of time.

      


      
      

        Try it
        
Move 3: Average over time · Version 4

        

        
Let each speed expire

        

          

            

              
average.jssetTimeout is new

              

            

          

          

            

            
            

              
0speeds.length

              
–average

            

            
setTimeout(forgetOldest, 500): call forgetOldest once, half a second from now.

          

        

        
The fix: every speed stays for half a second, then leaves. Each push also sets a timer: setTimeout(forgetOldest, 500) asks the browser to call forgetOldest once, 500 ms later. New: setTimeout. It's like addEventListener, but the event is "half a second has passed". Every speed has the same lifetime and they arrive in order, so the one leaving is always the oldest, at the front: shift. Because speeds now also leave when nothing moves, both functions call useAverage, which works out the average and sets the detune. When the array is empty, the average is 0. Flick and stop with Half a second: the bars cross the dashed line and disappear, the average falls to 0, the note returns. Switch to Keep 10 and do the same: the bars stay, the note stays bent. Moving slowly or quickly, the average now always covers the same half second. Act it out if there's time: students hold speed cards and each one sits down half a second after standing up.

Wrap-up

          
Normalise. Speed. Average over time.

          
0 to 1 means the same on any screen, and stretches onto whatever the sound needs. Speed is worked out from two moves. An average over a short, recent window smooths it, and lets it settle when you stop.

          
clientX / window.innerWidthnormX * 1200Math.hypot(…)distance / secondsspeeds.push(speed)speeds.shift()setTimeout(forgetOldest, 500)

        

        
11:50. The three moves. Normalise: divide by the window size so 0 is one edge and 1 the other, then stretch or squeeze that onto the range the sound needs. Speed: compare this position with the last one, in screens per second. Average over time: one move is choppy; averaging all of history gets sluggish; the last 10 is better; keeping only 10 is simpler; letting each value expire after half a second also settles when the pointer stops.
